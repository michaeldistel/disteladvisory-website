# The night phone line is the first job I'd give an AI voice agent

At five o'clock a lift maintenance firm in England diverts its phones to an answering
service. The operator who picks up at night takes a name, a number and a short message,
emails it to the office, texts the engineer on call and then rings the engineer to check
they're going. The firm pays for each attempt to reach an engineer. Every Monday the
service manager emails over a new Excel file with that week's rota, and every Monday
somebody hopes it was opened.

I work very closely with [Vocals](https://usevocals.com), a company that builds AI voice agents, on their clients'
projects and on building the product itself. This year I've sat in on calls like that one
with lift firms in the UK and Spain, and the setup barely changes. A Spanish firm with
around a dozen branches sends every call outside office hours to one external call
centre. The person we spoke to there put routine data capture and routing at about 95 per
cent of those calls.

Large firms in Singapore have already moved. In March 2026 Singtel said its AI assistant
had handled more than 70,000 cases in a six-week pilot and resolved 73 per cent of mobile
and home troubleshooting requests without a person. Taco Bell went the other way. It put
voice ordering into more than 500 US drive-throughs from 2023, and in August 2025 told
the Wall Street Journal it was rethinking where to use it, after customers posted videos
of the system taking an order for 18,000 cups of water.

I think the difference is the shape of the call. A drive-through order is open-ended, and
the customer is free to mess about. A night call to a lift firm is narrow. The agent
needs to know who's calling, which site, what's wrong and whether anyone's trapped, and
then it has to find the right engineer. That's where an agent beats the call centre,
because it can read the rota where the firm already keeps it, call the engineer, try the
next name if nobody answers, and send the office a summary before anyone's out of bed.

The part I'd hold firm on is what the agent doesn't do. On our first lift project,
entrapment calls stay with the existing service in phase one. They were about 4 per cent of calls in the client's own log, and the agent's job is to recognise one and transfer it.
We also don't ask callers whether they want to complain, because the question suggests
complaints are common. The agent listens for it and books a callback from a person the
next working day. A sceptical branch manager told our team that callers can always tell
it's an AI. I think that's fine, as long as the caller who needs a person gets one
quickly.

My clients don't fix lifts, but most of them have the same night line. In a logistics
firm it's the operations manager's mobile, or a duty phone passed round the team each
week. The calls are a driver turned away at a port gate, a customer chasing a delivery
order, or an agent in another time zone asking about a container. Most could wait until
morning and a few need a person within the hour, but somebody senior gets woken up for
all of them.

Before you buy anything, take three months of the calls that came in after hours and sort
them into the ones that needed a person that night and the ones that could have waited.
Then write down where the on-call rota actually lives. If the answer is a WhatsApp
message from last Monday, fix that first, because no agent can route calls to a rota
nobody can find.

When we set one up, it starts with the night line only and sends anything the client
calls an emergency straight to a person.
