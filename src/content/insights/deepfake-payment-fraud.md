# A video call no longer proves who is asking for the money

The finance manager at a trading firm gets a WhatsApp message from the managing
director, who is overseas. A supplier deal needs a confidential deposit today, and
there'll be a short video call to confirm it. The managing director is on the call, a
little out of focus on a hotel connection, with a lawyer she hasn't met before. The
amount is large but not unusual for the firm, so she makes the transfer before the end
of the day.

I hear versions of this from clients, and until recently the video call was the part
that made it safe. If you could see and hear the person, it was them.

On 14 May 2026 the Singapore Police Force [reported](https://www.police.gov.sg/media-hub/news/2026/05/20260514_advisory_on_scams_involving_impersonation_of_snr_govt_officials_incl_secy_cabinet) that a businessman had lost at least
S$4.9 million after a Zoom meeting with deepfakes of Prime Minister Lawrence Wong and
other officials. The invitation came by WhatsApp, from an account showing the photo of
the Secretary to the Cabinet. A year earlier, on 25 March 2025, the finance director of
a multinational joined a Zoom call in which the chief executive and others were
impersonated "using deepfake technology". He had already been called by a supposed
lawyer and had signed a non-disclosure agreement. The next day he sent over US$499,000
to a money mule account, and only realised when he was asked for US$1.4 million more.
Police in Singapore and Hong Kong traced and held the money. Arup was less lucky in
2024, when a finance employee in its Hong Kong office made 15 transfers totalling
HK$200 million after a call on which, in the words of the Hong Kong police, everyone he
saw was fake.

I think the important change is the price. A convincing copy of your managing director
needs a few minutes of public video, from a trade event or a podcast, and a tool anyone
can sign up for online. The police published the footage of the
Prime Minister call and pointed out the lip movements that didn't match the audio.
That's useful this year. The tools that come out next year won't have that flaw, so
teaching staff to spot fakes is a contest a finance team will eventually lose.

My clients pay suppliers and agents overseas every week, often under time pressure, in
amounts where US$499,000 doesn't look strange. For them the risk is rarely the Prime
Minister. It's the managing director on WhatsApp, or a supplier's email saying the bank
account has changed, now with a voice note or a video call behind it.

What works doesn't depend on recognising anyone. Any new payee or change of bank
details is confirmed by calling a number already on file, never one in the message.
Anything above a set amount needs a second approver. And the directors tell the finance
team in writing that they will never ask for a confidential payment outside the normal
process, which takes away the secrecy both Singapore cases relied on. None of this needs
a consultant, and it can be in place by Friday.

An invoice-matching agent helps here too, because it compares the bank details on every
supplier invoice with the vendor master and holds the payment when they differ. A person
skips that check on a busy afternoon, and the agent doesn't.

payment rule under which it doesn't matter whether the face on the call is real. If
